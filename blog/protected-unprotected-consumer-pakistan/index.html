Your neighbour used 180 units last month. So did you. Their bill came to roughly Rs 2,900 and yours to roughly Rs 5,900. Nothing about the wiring explains that. The reason is a single word printed in the consumer block of your bill: protected, or unprotected.

Most people never look at that line. Then one summer month pushes them over 200 units. The bill stays high for half a year, even after usage drops back. This guide shows you where the line sits. It explains what the rule actually says. And it covers the three wrong versions that circulate online.

The gap

The gap between protected and unprotected

Two categories, same electricity, very different arithmetic. A protected home pays Rs 10.54 for each of its first 100 units. An unprotected home pays Rs 22.44 for those identical units. Above 100 units the gap widens again: Rs 13.01 against Rs 28.91.

What the same units cost in each category
Monthly unitsProtected rateUnprotected rateFixed charge per kW
1 to 100Rs 10.54Rs 22.44Rs 200 / Rs 275
101 to 200Rs 13.01Rs 28.91Rs 300 / Rs 300
201 to 300not availableRs 33.10Rs 350

Read that middle row again. A home using 150 units pays Rs 13.01 per unit if protected and Rs 28.91 if not. That is Rs 2,387 against Rs 4,337 in energy charges alone, before a single tax is added.

There is a second advantage that gets less attention. Protected homes get the benefit of the previous slab, so their first 100 units stay at the cheaper rate. Unprotected homes pay every unit at the rate of the slab they land in. Yehi wajah hai ke farq itna bara lagta hai.

The rule

The real rule: 200 units, six months, every month

Here is the definition as it is actually notified. FESCO's tariff terms of 13 January 2026, at Annex V, describe the protected category in one sentence. It is non-time-of-use residential consumption. And it means no more than 200 kWh in each month, consistently, across the preceding six months. Residential non-ToU consumers who fall outside that description are unprotected.

The same six-month structure appears in NEPRA's earlier notification, S.R.O. 752(I)/2022, which recorded that the protected category was created for consumers consuming up to 200 units for six months. K-Electric's published explanation, notified on 5 November 2021, describes it the same way.

Three parts of that sentence do the work:

  • Non-ToU. The test applies to ordinary residential connections. Time-of-use meters run on a different schedule.
  • No more than 200 kWh. Exactly 200 qualifies. The wording is inclusive.
  • Consistently, for six months. Each month is judged on its own. An average will not do.

Your DISCO reviews the category monthly against your meter history. Cross 200 once and you are reclassified. Getting back takes six clean months.

Myths

Three things that are widely repeated and wrong

This is where most of the confusion comes from. Three claims circulate on bill-checking sites, and two of them will cost you money if you believe them.

Myth 1: registering an air conditioner disqualifies you permanently

Several sites state that any AC registered against your meter's sanctioned load removes protected status permanently, whatever you actually consume. We could not find this condition in any tariff document. The notified definition rests on non-ToU status and six months of consumption at or below 200 kWh.

We are not saying those sites are lying. We are saying the rule as notified does not contain that clause, so do not restructure your life around it. If a DISCO official tells you otherwise, ask which notification they are reading. Polite question, useful answer.

Myth 2: it is a twelve-month average

Wrong, and easy to see why it spreads. A twelve-month test does exist in Pakistan's tariff history, but it belongs to lifeline, and the threshold there was 100 units, not 200. Someone merged the two rules somewhere along the way.

Protected status tests each month individually. One month at 260 units and five at 150 average out to 168, comfortably under the limit. That household still loses protected status, because the 260-unit month breaks the consistency requirement on its own.

Myth 3: the higher rate applies only in the month you cross

This is the most expensive one to believe. The higher rate applies in the month you cross 200 units. It then applies for the following five months, even if your usage falls straight back to 150. We work out exactly what that costs in the section below.

On your bill

Find your status on the bill

Open any recent bill, paper or PDF. Look for the consumer block near your name and address. It is the same area that holds the reference number and the meter number. Your tariff code sits there.

Where the category appears
Bill areaWhat to look for
Tariff codeA-1 for residential. Some bills add a P or the word Protected.
Consumer blockA status line reading Protected or Unprotected near the code.
Reverse of a paper billThe printed slab table. It matches whichever category you are in.
Online duplicate billSame fields. Open the current month, not an old one.

Codes vary by company, which is half the problem. Some DISCOs print a clear word, some print only a code, and a few print nothing obvious at all. Do not guess from a single low bill. Pull the current month and read what is printed. Agar aapko bill online check karna hai, reference number se official portal par current copy khulegi.

Four-step check that works anywhere:

  1. Open the newest bill and copy the tariff code and the status line.
  2. Note your sanctioned load in kW, printed in the same block.
  3. List your last six months of units with their reading dates.
  4. Ask whether every one of those six sits at 200 or below.

If step four comes back yes and your bill still says unprotected, read on. You may be one of the households in the next section.

Cost of a spike

What one heavy month actually costs

Take a household that normally runs at 150 units a month. In June the heat pushes it to 210. That is 60 extra units, one time.

Here is the six-month consequence. It is worked on the schedule our calculator follows. Fuel adjustment, quarterly adjustment, electricity duty, GST and the TV fee are all included.

Look at July onwards. The household is back to exactly 150 units, the same as always, and still paying Rs 3,152 more every month. That is the part people do not expect.

So 60 extra units in one month cost about Rs 1,800 of electricity at unprotected rates. By the time the six months run out, they have cost Rs 22,318. The units are not the cost. The reclassification is. You can run the same arithmetic on your own numbers with the bill calculator.

One caveat, and it matters. These figures leave out the financing cost surcharge of roughly Rs 3.23 per unit that appears on some 2026 bills. Your real total will sit a little higher. Ye estimate hai, final bill nahi.

Wrongly billed

About 2.4 million homes are billed as unprotected when they qualify

This is the number that should bother you. Ministry of Energy figures recorded in NEPRA's decision of 11 February 2026 put protected households at roughly 20.2 million in 2026. That is up from about 7.9 million in 2022. The same table counts around 2.4 million households using 200 units or less that are still billed as unprotected.

Some of those will have a genuine reason, a ToU meter or a commercial tariff on the connection. Two and a half million is too many for that alone. The common causes are worth knowing:

  • A stretched billing cycle. A 40-day reading period collects more units than a 30-day one, and can carry you over 200 on its own.
  • An estimated reading that was never replaced by an actual one.
  • A name change or meter change that reset the account history in the system.
  • A tariff code that was never updated after a load change.

If your six months are genuinely all at or below 200 and the bill still says unprotected, that is worth querying. The next section gives you the specific rule to quote.

Late reading

A late meter reading does not have to cost you your status

NEPRA's Consumer Service Manual, revised in 2025 and circulated on 26 November 2025, covers a long billing cycle. It applies when the cycle runs long because of force majeure or an unforeseen situation. Section 6.1.1.1 describes pro-rating the units to a normal month.

The manual's own example makes it concrete. A reading of 208 units taken over 33 days works out to 189 units for a 30-day month. Because 189 sits under 200, the first 189 units are charged at protected rates. The extra 19 are carried into the next bill. And your protected status stays intact.

Section 6.1.1.2 says the carried units must be shown separately on the bill, so you can check it was done.

This is a specific rule for an abnormal cycle, not a licence to pick your favourite reading period. But if your bill jumped after a long gap between readings, this is exactly the provision to raise. Record the scheduled and actual reading dates, the billed units, and any carry-forward line before you contact the office. Our guide to missing and delayed bills covers the evidence trail.

Getting it back

Getting protected status back

Six consecutive months at 200 units or below. There is no shortcut and no form that overrides the history.

The count starts from the month after the spike, so a June overshoot usually means protection returns with the January bill. Track it yourself rather than trusting the system to notice. Keep every bill or a photograph of the meter reading on the same date each month.

Practical margin helps. If you are averaging 185 units, a single visitor-heavy week can undo five months of care. Aim for 170 if you can, and be strict in the two months before summer. I have watched people lose it in the last week of a clean run, which hurts.

Lifeline

Lifeline, protected and unprotected compared

Lifeline is not a lower version of protected. It is a separate category with its own test, and a small bill does not put you in it. Do not assume the cheapest rate applies just because your usage looks low.

K-Electric

K-Electric: same idea, different system

Karachi runs on its own billing platform, not the PITC portal the eleven DISCOs share. You check a KE bill with the 13-digit account number rather than a 14-digit reference number.

The definition travels across, though. K-Electric says the same thing in its published explanation of tariff terms. Protected customers are non-time-of-use residential. They use no more than 200 units in each month of the preceding six. The six-month structure holds; only the lookup route differs. Full detail in our K-Electric bill guide.

Solar homes

Solar and net-metering homes

Net metering changes what the units on your bill mean. Your billed figure is imports minus exports. A home that generates during the day can show a low number. It may still draw heavily at night.

The consumption test applies to the net billed units. If those stay at 200 or below across six months, the usage condition is met. The complication is your sanctioned load. Most net-metered homes run a load well above 1 kW. The tariff terms tie ToU metering to loads of 5 kW and above. A ToU connection does not use the same protected test at all.

If you have solar and a ToU meter, check which arrangement your account is actually on before applying any of this. The net metering guide covers the February 2026 rule change and what it does to the units on your bill.

January 2027

What may change in January 2027

Two separate things are moving, and they point in different directions. Neither has been notified, so neither has changed anyone's bill yet.

Current rule against what is proposed
Applies nowProposed
Consequence of crossing 200Higher rate for six monthsHigher rate for that month only
Who proposed it—Power Division, reported 21 September 2026
StatusIn forceIMF agreement pending, no notification
The subsidy itselfUniform cross-subsidyIMF has asked for BISP targeting from January 2027

The relief proposal is narrow. ProPakistani, Dunya News and Pakistan Observer reported this on 21 September 2026. It would not change the protected rates or the 200-unit limit. It also would not change what you pay in the month you go over. It would only remove the five further months at unprotected rates. The National Assembly Standing Committee on Power endorsed the change on 17 September. That endorsement is a recommendation, not a rule.

Then on 5 October 2026, Daily Ittehad reported something larger. The IMF has asked Pakistan to remove the cross-subsidy for consumers using up to 200 units. The government proposes BISP-targeted assistance instead, from January 2027. The IMF flagged Rs 1,675 billion in circular debt in the same context.

So one proposal would soften the rule and the other would replace the subsidy behind it. Both need a notification before anything reaches your bill. Until then, the six-month rule stands and the planning above is worth doing.

Disputes

If your status looks wrong

Go to your sub-division office, or write to them. A focused request gets investigated faster than a general complaint about a big bill.

State four things. The bill month. The printed category. The units for each of the six months you believe were assessed. And which reading or classification you are disputing. Attach the bills. Ask them to explain which monthly records were used.

Something like: please explain which monthly records were used to classify this account, and review the attached reading for June.

Three things to keep straight. A complaint does not pause your due date, so ask in writing about payment while it is reviewed. A ticket number proves only that you filed something, so check the next bill for the corrected category. And a request to reduce accurately recorded consumption below the threshold is not the same as correcting a genuine error. Do not pay anyone who promises to change your status.

For charges that appear on top of the energy charge, the NEPRA tariff guide explains where each line comes from. The fixed charge guide covers the part of the bill that does not move with your units.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions

Is using fewer than 200 units this month enough?

No. The tariff definition also requires a non-time-of-use residential connection and a consistent history. Every one of the last six months has to sit at 200 units or below, not just the latest one.

Does exactly 200 units satisfy the limit?

Yes. FESCO's tariff terms notified on 13 January 2026 use the wording no more than 200 kWh. The other conditions still have to be met, and the six-month history still has to be clean.

Can I average six months to work out eligibility?

No. An average can hide a month above the limit. One month at 260 units and five at 150 average to 168, but that single month still breaks the consistency requirement.

Does installing an air conditioner end protected status?

We could not find any such condition in the tariff documents. The notified definition rests on non-ToU residential status and six months of consumption at or below 200 kWh. Some sites state an AC rule without citing a source, so treat it as unverified.

How long does it take to get protected status back?

Six consecutive months at 200 units or below. The count starts after the heavy month, so a June spike usually means protection returns with the January bill. Keep every bill as evidence.

Can I apply to change my category myself?

Not by request alone. The classification follows your recorded consumption history. What you can do is ask your DISCO to review the months it used. You can also ask it to correct a reading that is genuinely wrong.

Does K-Electric use the same test?

K-Electric's own published explanation describes protected customers the same way. Non-time-of-use residential. No more than 200 units in each month of the preceding six. Karachi bills still run on KE's separate system, not the PITC portal.

Is the protected category being abolished in January 2027?

Nothing has been notified. Two things are in play. A Power Division proposal to end the six-month penalty. And an IMF request to move the subsidy to BISP targeting from January 2027. Neither changes your bill until a notification is issued.
