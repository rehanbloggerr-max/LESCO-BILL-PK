Fixed charges on an electricity bill: what you pay in 2026

A charge that does not move when you switch things off. Every 2026 rate. The three rules behind the kilowatts you are billed on. And the one lever that can shrink it.

The flat stayed shut for a month. No fan, no lights, no television. Then the bill arrived anyway, and it was not a small one either.

That is the fixed charge. Since February 2026, fixed charges on an electricity bill have applied to almost every domestic connection in Pakistan. They are the one line that does not care how much electricity you used.

This guide sets out every rate. It explains the three rules behind the kilowatts you are billed on. And it names the one lever that can reduce it. All figures follow the schedule notified by NEPRA in SRO 279(I)/2026.

The basics

What the fixed charge on your bill actually is

Fixed charges on an electricity bill are a charge for capacity. Not for electricity. You pay per kilowatt of sanctioned load, every month. It is not a charge for electricity.

That distinction is the whole thing. The energy charge is what you pay for units, and it moves with every appliance you switch on. The fixed charge is what you pay for the size of the pipe into your house. It stays put whether or not anything flows through it.

The word "fixed" misleads people into thinking it is a flat fee, the same for everyone. It is not. It is fixed to your load. A 2 kW connection pays twice what a 1 kW connection pays in the same slab. A 4 kW connection pays four times as much.

Most unprotected homes pay between Rs 275 and Rs 675 for each kilowatt, every month, before a single unit is counted. Protected consumers pay less, and lifeline consumers pay nothing at all.

The shock

The bill you get for using nothing

Here is the scenario that sends people to the DISCO office. You were away for a month. The meter moved by zero units. The bill still came.

Run the arithmetic for a 1 kW unprotected connection that used no units at all. The schedule is the one our calculator follows.

A 1 kW unprotected connection that used no units at all
LineAmount
Energy charge (0 units)Rs 0
Fixed charge (1 kW × Rs 275)Rs 275.00
GST at 18%Rs 49.50
TV licence feeRs 35.00
Total payableRs 359.50

The energy charge is genuinely zero, because no units were consumed. Everything else is not. The fixed charge is Rs 275. GST at 18% takes Rs 49.50 on top of it. The Rs 35 TV licence fee is a flat government levy that never switches off.

So you get a bill of about Rs 360 for a month in which you used no electricity whatsoever. Work out a per-unit rate for that month and the answer is not a large number. It has no meaning at all, because you are dividing by zero.

A lifeline home in the same position pays only the Rs 35 TV fee. That is the exemption doing its work.

Run the same month for a protected consumer on a 2 kW connection and the shape changes again. The protected rate in the lowest slab is Rs 200 per kW. So the fixed charge comes to Rs 400. GST adds Rs 72. The TV fee stays at Rs 35. Rs 507 for a month of consuming nothing, on a connection twice the size.

Two things follow from this. The fixed charge scales with your load even when your consumption is zero. And your category, lifeline, protected or unprotected, changes the number more than anything else on the bill. It is the reason the next section is worth reading even if you already know your rates.

Find it

Where the fixed charge appears on your bill

Fixed charges on an electricity bill sit below the energy charge. Look in the block that lists your tariff and connection details. The charge is printed beside your sanctioned load in kW, and both figures appear together.

Find your meter number first, near the top of the bill. The sanctioned load, the tariff code and the fixed charge are all in that same neighbourhood. Some bills print the tariff code as A-1 for a normal domestic connection.

The label is not consistent across companies. You may see Fixed Charges, F.C., or simply Fixed Charge. All three mean the same line.

If you cannot find it on the paper copy, pull the duplicate bill from your company's page. The online version lays the same block out more cleanly. You can compare it against last month's figure, side by side.

Two things stand out here. Lifeline consumers are the only category with no fixed charge at all. And the unprotected rate makes its biggest jump at the 501 to 600 unit slab. It leaps from Rs 500 to Rs 675 per kW in one step.

What these rates replaced

Fixed charges are not new in themselves. What is new is how high they go, and the fact that protected consumers now pay them at all. The February 2026 decision roughly doubled the rates in the middle slabs while cutting them at the top.

What the February 2026 decision changed
Unprotected slabBefore Feb 2026Now
301–400 unitsRs 200 per kWRs 400 per kW
401–500 unitsRs 400 per kWRs 500 per kW
501–600 unitsRs 600 per kWRs 675 per kW
601–700 unitsRs 800 per kWRs 675 per kW
Above 700 unitsRs 1,000 per kWRs 675 per kW

Read the last two rows again, because they run against the grain of the whole change. The highest-consuming households actually saw their fixed charge fall. By Rs 125 per kW at 601 to 700 units. By Rs 325 per kW above that. The rises landed in the middle.

That is why the reaction has been so sharp. A household at 350 units now pays twice the fixed charge it paid in January. A household at 800 units pays less than a third of what it used to.

Wrong numbers still circulating online

One widely-read article from April 2026 gets the middle slabs wrong. It lists them at Rs 400, Rs 550, Rs 600 and Rs 650 per kW. Those figures are wrong. Four independent sources agree on the correct schedule. NEPRA's own SRO 279(I)/2026 notification. The published IESCO tariff guide. And two regularly updated tariff trackers.

The mechanism

How the kW figure is decided

This is the part almost no explanation covers, and it is where the surprises live. The number of kilowatts you are actually billed on depends on what kind of connection you have.

How the billed kilowatts are decided
ConnectionBilled on
Domestic, normal slab tariffSanctioned load
Domestic time-of-use, 5 kW and above50% of load or MDI, whichever is higher
Commercial, up to 5 kWFlat Rs 1,000, load irrelevant
Commercial, above 5 kW25% of load or MDI, whichever is higher

MDI means maximum demand indicator. It is the highest demand your meter recorded at any point during the month.

Take a shop with a 10 kW sanctioned load. A quarter of that is 2.5 kW. If the meter peaked at 4 kW during the month, the fixed charge is worked out on 4 kW. That is the higher of the two. At Rs 1,250 per kW that is Rs 5,000 for the month.

The same logic applies to a domestic time-of-use connection, and this one catches people out. Take a home with a 5 kW sanctioned load on a ToU meter. Half of that is 2.5 kW. If the meter recorded a peak of 3.2 kW that month, the fixed charge is worked out on 3.2 kW. Not on the sanctioned 5 kW. Not on the 2.5 kW half. At Rs 675 per kW, that is Rs 2,160 for the month.

So the domestic ToU rule cuts both ways. It can work out below the full sanctioned load, which is a relief for bigger connections. But it also means the figure can climb month to month even though nothing about your connection changed. One hot evening when everything ran at once sets a new peak, and the fixed charge follows it up.

Worked through

Worked examples: 1 kW, 3 kW and 5 kW

Three homes, all using exactly 250 units in the same month. All three land in the 201 to 300 unit slab. So all three pay the same Rs 33.10 per unit. And the same Rs 350 per kW fixed charge. The only thing that differs is the sanctioned load.

Same 250 units, three different sanctioned loads
Sanctioned loadEnergy charge (250 units)Fixed chargeTotal before tax
1 kWRs 8,275Rs 350Rs 8,625
3 kWRs 8,275Rs 1,050Rs 9,325
5 kWRs 8,275Rs 1,750Rs 10,025

Look at the energy charge column. It does not move. All three homes used the same 250 units, so all three owe the same Rs 8,275 in energy charges.

Now look at the fixed charge column. It triples, then nearly quintuples. The 5 kW home pays Rs 1,400 more than the 1 kW home for using the identical amount of electricity.

Over a year that gap is Rs 16,800. This is the multiplier effect that catches people out. Two neighbours using the same units every month can carry permanently different bills. Purely because of the load their connection was sanctioned for.

It also explains a common complaint. Someone switches to LED bulbs, cuts their units by a third, and the bill barely moves. The units fell. The fixed charge did not.

The reasoning

Why NEPRA introduced it

The regulator's argument rests on one structural fact about the power sector. Roughly 73% of the grid's total cost base is fixed in nature. Capacity payments, transmission infrastructure and network maintenance cost money whether or not anyone draws a single unit.

Under the old structure, almost all of that was recovered through per-unit charges. NEPRA's view was that this mispriced the system. Moving part of the recovery onto fixed charges reflects the real cost structure.

There is a second, larger purpose. The recalibrated fixed charges are projected to recover about Rs 132 billion a year. Of that, roughly Rs 101 billion removes the cross-subsidy that industry had been carrying. It would cut industrial tariffs by about Rs 4.04 per unit.

Stated plainly: the change makes industry more competitive, and it puts a fixed floor under every household. Around 28.5 million domestic consumers are affected.

Whether that trade-off was the right call is precisely what the next section is about.

Still open

The review petition: is the fixed charge legal?

Yes, in the only sense that matters for paying your bill. It was notified by the Ministry of Energy in SRO 279(I)/2026 on 12 February 2026, following NEPRA's decision of 11 February. It is in force, and it stays in force unless the regulator recalls it.

It is also being challenged. A review petition was filed with NEPRA and reported by Business Recorder on 11 March 2026. The lead petitioner is Lt Gen (retd) Moin ud Din Haider, a former interior minister, joined by four other consumers.

Their argument has three parts. The 73% figure is a system-wide statistic, not a class-by-class cost study showing that residential load should carry that share. The charge hits lower and middle income households hardest. A fixed unavoidable cost takes a bigger slice of a smaller budget. And the redesign functions as a redistribution toward industry, which they say required its own transparent justification.

They have asked NEPRA to recall the decision and suspend the charges while it is redetermined. They also want the full cost-of-service data published. And an affordability assessment run.

Status: pending as of the most recent reporting, 18 August 2026. We have found no published decision since. Confirm the current position on NEPRA's website before relying on it. Until something changes, the fixed charge is live and collectable.

Cleared up

Fixed charge vs meter rent vs minimum charge vs TV fee

Four separate lines appear near each other on the bill, and people mix them up constantly. Only one of them scales with your load. Knowing which is which is the difference between understanding fixed charges on an electricity bill and guessing at them.

Four lines people confuse
LineAmountDepends on
Fixed chargeRs 200–675 per kWSanctioned load and slab
Meter rentRs 75 or Rs 150Single or three phase
Minimum monthly chargeRs 75 or Rs 150A floor, not an addition
TV licence feeRs 35Every connection, flat

Here is the one-line test. If it grows when your kW grows, it is the fixed charge. Every other one is flat.

The minimum monthly charge is a floor, not an addition. If your computed charges come to less than Rs 75 on a single-phase connection, you pay Rs 75. It does not stack on top of the fixed charge.

Verify it

Is your sanctioned load recorded correctly?

Since the fixed charge multiplies your load, a wrongly recorded load is now an expensive error. This is the most valuable two minutes you can spend on the bill.

Find the sanctioned load printed in kW near your meter number. Most homes sit somewhere between 1 kW and 5 kW. Now compare it against the load on your connection sanction letter, or the figure in your original application.

Loads get recorded wrong more often than you would expect. An old application that was never updated. A load increase you never requested. A connection that changed hands and carried the previous occupant's load across.

What the error costs. Recorded at 4 kW when you applied for 3 kW? In a Rs 500 per kW slab, that is Rs 500 every month. Over a year, Rs 6,000, for a number typed in wrong.

If it does not match, take the bill and your sanction documents to the sub-division office. Ask for a load correction. If the company does not act, NEPRA's complaint route is the next step. The regulator's mobile app lets you register the complaint directly.

Also check your category, not just your load

The load sets how many kilowatts you pay for. Your category sets the rate on each of them, and the gap is large. A protected consumer pays Rs 300 per kW where an unprotected consumer at the same consumption pays Rs 350. Higher up the slabs the gap widens considerably.

Protected status means staying under 200 units for six consecutive months. No air conditioner registered against the connection. And a sanctioned load below 5 kW.

The catch is that the status is applied by looking backwards. Your bill header prints Protected or Unprotected near the tariff code, based on the preceding six months. A single heavy summer flips you to unprotected for the months that follow. The fixed charge rate moves with it. Check the word on every bill, not just the first one you look at.

The lever

Can you reduce your fixed charge?

Let us take the disappointing answer first. Using less electricity does not reduce fixed charges on an electricity bill at all. That is the most common question, and the honest answer is no.

The load is the only lever, and there are three ways to move it.

  1. Check the recorded load is right. A correction is free, and it is the single highest-value check on this page. See the section above.
  2. Apply to reduce your sanctioned load if your real peak demand sits well below it. Many homes were sanctioned for a load they have never come close to drawing.
  3. Check whether you qualify for protected status. Under 200 units for six straight months? No air conditioner registered? A sanctioned load under 5 kW moves you onto the lower protected rates.

One warning before you apply for a reduction. Dropping to a load below 5 kW moves you off the time-of-use tariff and onto the slab tariff. For some households that is a saving, for others it is not. Work out both before you file anything.

And set your expectations honestly. This is a paperwork route with a wait attached. It is not a quick fix, but it is the only one there is.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions

What is the fixed charge on my electricity bill in Pakistan?

It is a monthly charge on your sanctioned load, not on the units you use. For most unprotected homes it runs Rs 275 to Rs 675 for each kilowatt. Lifeline consumers pay no fixed charge at all.

Why am I being charged when I used no electricity?

Because the fixed charge bills your connection's capacity, not your consumption. A home that used zero units in a month still pays the fixed charge. Plus the TV licence fee. Plus GST on top of both.

How is the fixed charge calculated?

Multiply the rate for your slab by the kilowatts you are billed on. A 3 kW connection in the 201 to 300 unit slab pays Rs 350 times three, which is Rs 1,050 for the month.

How do I find my sanctioned load?

It is printed on the bill near the meter number and the tariff code, given in kW. Most homes sit between 1 kW and 5 kW. Your connection sanction letter shows the same figure.

Do protected consumers pay the fixed charge?

Yes, at lower rates. Protected consumers pay Rs 200 per kW up to 100 units and Rs 300 per kW from 101 to 200 units. Only lifeline consumers are fully exempt.

Are lifeline consumers exempt from fixed charges?

Yes. Lifeline consumers, those using up to 100 units a month under the lifeline slabs, pay no fixed charge. They still pay the Rs 35 TV licence fee.

Can I reduce my fixed charge?

Not by using less electricity. The only lever is the load itself. Check your recorded sanctioned load is correct. Apply to reduce it if your real peak demand sits well below.

Is the fixed charge legal? Is it being challenged?

It was notified and it stands. A review petition filed with NEPRA in March 2026 asks the regulator to recall it. That petition was still pending as of the most recent reporting in August 2026. Check NEPRA for the current status.
