Net metering in Pakistan: what changed, and what your bill looks like now

NEPRA replaced net metering with net billing in February 2026. One home in our worked example went from a zero energy charge to Rs 50,328 a year. Same panels, same roof.

Your neighbour used 180 units last month. So did you. Their bill came to roughly Rs 2,900 and yours to roughly Rs 5,900. Nothing about the wiring explains that. The reason is a single word printed in the consumer block of your bill: protected, or unprotected.

Most people never look at that line. Then one summer month pushes them over 200 units. The bill stays high for half a year, even after usage drops back. This guide shows you where the line sits. It explains what the rule actually says. And it covers the three wrong versions that circulate online.

More

The short answer

Yes, you can get it back. The official CCMS portal at ccms.pitc.com.pk verifies you by registered mobile number or by CNIC. It then lists your connections with the reference number beside each one. That single route solves the problem for most people in about five minutes, and it costs nothing.

Seven more routes sit behind it. Some are faster if you happen to have a particular scrap of paper. Some are the only option left when the account was registered decades ago under a relative's name. This guide covers all eight, in the order worth trying.

One clarification up front, because it confuses almost everyone. You cannot check a bill using a CNIC. The PITC bill portal accepts a reference number or a customer ID and nothing else. But you can use a CNIC to recover the reference number. Same card, two completely different questions. Most guides online give one answer to both and call it a day.

The short version

If you installed solar before 9 February 2026, your signed agreement still governs. Your old buyback rate holds until that agreement expires. Nothing in this guide takes that away from you.

If you are installing now, the rules are different. You will be paid a wholesale benchmark for what you export and charged the full retail rate for what you import. Those two numbers sit far apart, and the gap is the whole story.

We are not a solar company and we do not sell panels. This site explains bills. So this guide does what the sales pages generally do not. It shows the worked arithmetic. It explains why two different buyback rates are quoted online. And it is honest about the parts of this policy that are still unsettled.

What changed

What happened in February 2026

On 9 February 2026, NEPRA notified the Prosumer Regulations 2026 through SRO 251(I)/2026. The rules took effect the same day. They repeal the 2015 net metering framework that ran for a decade.

The regulations cover solar, wind and biogas systems up to 1 megawatt connected to a distribution network. Generate your own power and send the surplus to the grid? The rules now call you a prosumer. The old term of art, net metering, is gone from the statute.

Three things changed at once. The export rate moved from the retail tariff to a wholesale benchmark. The standard agreement dropped from seven years to five. And a non-refundable concurrence fee of Rs 1,000 per kW appeared where there was none before. We will take each of these in turn.

One clarification before anything else. Nothing here bans rooftop solar. You can still install a system, connect it and export your surplus. What changed is what that surplus is worth. Aur yehi farq aapke bill par nazar aata hai.

The mechanism

Net metering and net billing are not the same thing

The old system was beautifully simple. Your meter ran backwards when you exported and forwards when you imported. At the end of the month the company read one number. If you exported 360 units and imported 260, you owed nothing and carried 100 units forward.

Under net billing the two directions are counted separately and priced differently. Every unit you take from the grid is billed at your normal slab rate. Every unit you push back is credited at a much lower wholesale rate. The gap between those two numbers is what you pay.

The same home under both systems
 Net metering (old)Net billing (2026)
Exported unit is worthOne imported unitA fixed wholesale rate
Import charged atNet difference onlyFull slab rate
Agreement term7 years5 years
Concurrence feeNoneRs 1,000 per kW
Interconnection costMostly the companyThe prosumer
Selling to a neighbourNot allowedNot allowed

Read the second row again, because it carries most of the financial impact. Under the old rules your imported units were cancelled out by your exported ones. Now they are billed in full and the export only knocks a fraction off the total.

Two figures

Rs 8.13 or Rs 10 to 11? Both are right

Search for the new buyback rate and you will find two figures quoted with equal confidence. Solar installers quote one. Newspapers quote the other. Plenty of readers assume one of them is simply wrong. Neither is.

Where each figure comes from
FigureWhat it isWho uses it
Rs 8.13 per unitThe specific NAEPP figure in IESCO's February circularIESCO circular, Tribune, Profit
Rs 10 to 11 per unitThe same benchmark, rounded for readabilityDAWN and several solar sites
Rs 25 to 27 per unitThe old rate under signed agreementsApplies to existing prosumers only

The mechanism behind both numbers is the National Average Energy Purchase Price. It is what the system pays, on average, for a unit of energy. IESCO's internal circular of 25 February 2026 put that at Rs 8.13. Press coverage on 10 February rounded the same benchmark to Rs 10 to 11 because a range reads better than a precise decimal.

So when two pages disagree, they are usually reporting the same underlying number at different precision. What matters more than the decimal is this: it moves. NEPRA kept the power to revise purchase rates during the life of an agreement. Confirm the current figure with your own company before you sign anything.

NAEPP

What NAEPP actually measures

NAEPP stands for the National Average Energy Purchase Price. It is the blended average of what the power system pays generators. That covers every source: hydel, nuclear, coal, gas, furnace oil, wind and imports.

That is why it sits so far below your retail tariff. Your bill includes generation plus transmission, distribution, losses, capacity payments to independent producers and a stack of taxes. NAEPP covers generation only.

The practical consequence is a structural gap you cannot design around. Your exported unit is valued at the generation cost. Your imported unit is priced at the full retail rate. The two were never going to be equal.

Existing systems

If you already have solar, read this first

Existing prosumers were grandfathered, and the protection is real. But it has edges, and the edges are where people get caught.

What your existing agreement does and does not protect
SituationWhich rules apply
Signed before 9 February 2026Old rate until the agreement expires
Agreement reaches expiryCompany may move you to net billing
You add panels after that dateThe added capacity uses the new rate
You move houseThe connection stays; check the name transfer

The trap in adding capacity

That third row surprises people. Your original 5 kW array keeps its protected terms and its old buyback rate. Add another 3 kW next year and the new panels earn the NAEPP rate, not the old one. Two rates on one roof is now entirely possible.

Keep your signed agreement somewhere safe and photograph it. When a rate dispute comes up, that document is the whole argument. Aksar log isko ghar mein kahin rakh kar bhool jate hain, aur phir recover karna mushkil ho jata hai.

Solar homes also need to watch their tariff category separately. Recorded consumption often drops after panels go in. That can move a household out of the protected consumer category. The fixed charge does not disappear either.

New costs

What a new system costs before it earns anything

Three charges now sit between you and a connected system. None of them existed in this form under the 2015 rules.

Upfront costs for a new prosumer
ChargeAmountNotes
Concurrence feeRs 1,000 per kWNon-refundable, applies at any size
Bi-directional meterBorne by the prosumerOr a pair of one-way meters
Grid upgrade if neededBorne by the prosumerTransformer or line work

On a 5 kW system the concurrence fee alone is Rs 5,000. That is small next to the panels, but it is new and it is not refundable if your application stalls. NEPRA confirmed in April 2026 that concurrence is now required for every new prosumer, however small the installation.

The cost nobody quotes you

The meter and upgrade costs are the unpredictable part. Most homes need nothing beyond the meter. A property on a weak transformer can be asked to fund an upgrade first. That quote usually arrives after you have bought the panels.

Bill maths

What this does to an actual bill

Here is a worked example, because the percentages hide how much this matters. Take a home using 500 units a month with a 5 kW rooftop system generating about 600 units. Roughly 40 percent of that generation is used while the sun is up, so 240 units never reach the grid.

That leaves 260 units imported and 360 units exported each month.

Same home, same system, two rulebooks
 Net meteringNet billing at Rs 8.13
Units imported260260, billed in full
Units exported360360, credited at Rs 8.13
Import chargeOffset entirelyRs 7,121
Export creditCancels the importRs 2,927
Energy chargeRs 0Rs 4,194
Over a yearRs 0Rs 50,328

Under the old rules this home paid nothing for energy and banked 100 units for the following month. Under the new rules it pays Rs 4,194 a month, which is Rs 50,328 a year. Same panels, same roof, same metre of cable.

What these figures leave out

One honest caveat. These are energy charges only. Your bill will still carry the fixed charge per kW, electricity duty, GST and the TV licence fee. The fuel adjustment stays too. Solar reduces the energy line; it never takes the bill to zero. Both figures also assume the Rs 8.13 NAEPP, so check the current number with your own company before relying on them. You can run your own consumption through the bill calculator to see what your import side costs before the credit.

Payback

Why installers now quote 10 to 12 years

The payback period is the number that decides whether someone installs. Under the old rules, export-heavy systems paid for themselves in three to five years. Every exported unit was worth a full retail unit. That arithmetic no longer holds.

The wider context explains the mood. Power Division data puts grid-connected net metering solar at around 7,000 MW, with a further estimated 13,000 to 14,000 MW running off-grid. Roughly 466,000 consumers hold net metering connections. The off-grid figure is close to double the connected one, and that gap is the market voting with its feet.

Payback now depends almost entirely on how much of your generation you use yourself. A system sized to your daytime load still pays back respectably. A system sized to maximise exports does not, because exports earn roughly a fifth of what imports cost.

Self-consumption

The 4.8x rule that should drive your design

This is the single most useful number in the whole change, and it is simple to state.

What one unit is worth, depending on where it goes
Where the unit goesWhat it is worth
Consumed in your house while the sun is upRs 38.95 saved, at the top slab
Exported to the grid at Rs 8.13Rs 8.13 earned
Difference4.8 times

A unit you consume yourself is worth nearly five times a unit you sell. That single ratio should decide your system size, your panel orientation and whether a battery makes sense.

What buyers are doing differently

It explains three shifts in how people are buying. Systems are being sized to daytime load rather than to roof area. Battery storage has moved from a luxury to a serious line item, because evening consumption is the expensive half. Load shifting matters now. Running the washing machine, the iron and the water pump in daylight beats any export tariff. Yehi sab se bara behavioural change hai jo is rule ne laya hai.

The process

Getting connected: the shape of the process

The route from application to a working connection has more steps than most quotes suggest. Expect the following order.

  1. Apply to your DISCO with the system size, panel and inverter specifications, and a single-line diagram.
  2. Obtain NEPRA concurrence and pay the Rs 1,000 per kW fee. Nothing proceeds without this.
  3. Install the system to the approved specification, including the required protection equipment.
  4. Get the bi-directional meter fitted and any grid upgrade completed at your cost.
  5. Sign the prosumer agreement, now a five-year term renewable by mutual consent.

Two practical notes. Companies differ on how they sequence inspection and metering, so ask for the written sequence rather than assuming. And keep every receipt from the concurrence fee onward, because a dispute about capacity starts with paperwork.

IESCO circular

The eight design changes in IESCO's circular

An internal IESCO circular dated 25 February 2026 instructed field engineers to implement the new regulations in full. It listed eight design changes now in force for new consumers in its territory.

The change that stops most applications

The most consequential is the shift in the buyback benchmark. It moves from the Power Purchase Price to the lower Energy Purchase Price. Alongside it sit a revised installation limit and transformer capacity caps. The caps are the change most likely to stop an application on a dense urban street.

The remaining items cover the shortened five-year term, metering specifications and compliance requirements. Other companies are implementing the same regulations, though the local caps and limits differ. Ask your company for its own circular rather than relying on another territory's numbers.

Where it stands

Where the policy actually stands

This is the part most guides skip, and it matters because the rules are still moving. The sequence is worth knowing.

The regulations were notified on 9 February 2026. Within days the Prime Minister took notice and directed the Power Division to ask NEPRA to protect existing solar consumers. NEPRA then issued draft amendments on 16 February 2026 rolling back some of the changes for existing users.

Then the picture got messier. On 25 February, IESCO's internal circular told engineers to apply the regulations in full. That covered new connections, while the existing-consumer review was still unresolved. In public statements the power minister noted that the policy affects only about 1 percent of consumers. That argument drew its own share of criticism.

The honest position today

So the honest position as of October 2026 is this. New applicants are on net billing. Existing prosumers are protected under their signed agreements, and the draft amendments point toward keeping it that way. Nothing is fully settled, so verify the current rate and the current caps with your own company before committing money.

Other rates sit on top of the energy line. The NEPRA tariff guide explains who sets what and how often it moves. If your bill has climbed for reasons unrelated to solar, the diagnosis guide walks through the usual causes.

K-Electric

K-Electric runs its own system

Karachi is separate. K-Electric operates its own net metering framework, not the PITC-based route the others use. Its application forms, limits and rates are its own.

If you are in the KE territory, start with KE directly. Do not assume a DISCO rate, a DISCO capacity cap or a DISCO timeline applies to you. The direction of travel is similar across the country, but the paperwork is not. Our K-Electric bill guide covers where the account details sit and how KE statements differ from a PITC bill.

Safety

Three things worth being careful about

A rule change this large attracts confident advice, and some of it is expensive. Three checks keep you safe.

Verify the buyback rate with your company, not with a quotation. A proposal built on Rs 25 to 27 per unit is quoting the pre-February rate. Over five years the difference is not small.

Be sceptical of systems sized to your roof rather than to your load. Under net billing, capacity you export earns a fifth of what capacity you consume saves. Bigger is no longer better.

And do not pay anyone to handle your concurrence or your interconnection paperwork outside the official channel. The fee goes to NEPRA and the meter cost goes to the company. Both are payable against a receipt.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions

Has net metering been banned in Pakistan?

Not banned, but replaced for new applicants. NEPRA notified the Prosumer Regulations 2026 on 9 February 2026. They repeal the 2015 net metering framework and put new solar, wind and biogas systems up to 1 MW onto net billing. Existing signed agreements keep their original terms until they expire.

What is the new solar buyback rate?

New prosumers are paid the National Average Energy Purchase Price rather than the retail tariff. IESCO's internal circular and several outlets put that figure at Rs 8.13 per unit. Others round it to Rs 10 to 11. Both describe the same mechanism. Confirm the current figure with your own DISCO before you sign anything.

Why do some sites say Rs 8.13 and others say Rs 10-11?

They are reporting the same number at different levels of precision. Rs 8.13 is the specific NAEPP figure that appears in IESCO's February 2026 circular. Press coverage rounded it to a range for readability. Neither is a separate rate and neither applies to existing agreements.

What happens to my existing net metering agreement?

Nothing changes until it expires. Agreements signed before 9 February 2026 stay on their original terms and their original buyback rate. After expiry, your DISCO may move you onto the new net billing structure. Adding panels after that date puts only the added capacity under the new rules.

Can I add more panels to an existing system?

Yes, but the addition is treated separately. Your original system keeps its protected terms and old rate. The extra capacity falls under the 2026 net billing rules and earns the lower NAEPP rate. Tell your DISCO before you expand so both parts are recorded correctly.

What is the Rs 1,000 per kW fee?

It is a non-refundable concurrence fee that NEPRA introduced for new prosumers. You now need formal concurrence from the regulator regardless of how small your system is. On a 5 kW system that is Rs 5,000, on top of the meter and any grid upgrade the DISCO requires.

Is rooftop solar still worth it under net billing?

Often yes, but the maths changed. Exported units now earn roughly a fifth of what you pay to import them, so oversized systems lost their advantage. The saving comes from units you consume yourself while the sun is up. Size the system to your daytime load and consider battery storage for the evening.

Does K-Electric follow the same rules?

K-Electric runs its own net metering framework for Karachi rather than the PITC system. The direction of travel is the same, but the application route, forms and rates differ. Contact KE directly or read its published guidance before applying, and never assume a DISCO rate applies to a KE connection.
